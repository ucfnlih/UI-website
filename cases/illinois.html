Boone County, Illinois — Case Study

A plant went idle. Illinois barely noticed.

The Belvidere Assembly Plant was indefinitely idled on February 28, 2023, laying off more than a thousand manufacturing workers in a single month. In the state's official numbers, almost nothing happened.

What happened

The Stellantis Belvidere Assembly Plant, located in Belvidere, Boone County, Illinois, was indefinitely idled at the end of February 2023, resulting in large-scale layoffs of workers at the Jeep manufacturing facility. Because the plant was a major manufacturing employer in the local economy, the shutdown created a highly concentrated employment shock in Boone County and the broader Rockford metropolitan area, particularly in the manufacturing sector.

What follows are three ways of measuring that shock — by geography, by sector, and by data source — and what each one reveals or hides.

Evidence 01 — Geography

The shock fades as the map zooms out

Manufacturing unemployment risk, tracked at three levels of aggregation around the same event: Boone County itself, the Rockford metro area it belongs to, and the state of Illinois. Click a line in the legend to isolate it.

Unemployment risk · Manufacturing (NAICS 31) · monthly Oct 2022 – Oct 2023
8.8%Boone County, peak (May 2023)
4.8%Rockford metro, same month
1.7%Illinois, same month

At its peak, manufacturing unemployment risk in Boone County ran roughly five times the state figure and almost double the metro figure. Illinois's statewide manufacturing number moved by about a percentage point all year — the kind of month-to-month noise a state economist would not think twice about.

Evidence 02 — Sector

Even inside the county, blending sectors erases the signal

Boone County's economy is not only manufacturing. Combine every sector's claims and employment into one county-level number, and compare it against manufacturing alone.

Unemployment risk · Boone County · monthly Oct 2022 – Oct 2023
8.8%Manufacturing sector alone, peak
0.16%All sectors combined, same month
~56×how much the shock is diluted

Both lines sit on the same axis on purpose: at county-wide resolution, the shutdown all but disappears into a number fifty-some times smaller than the sector figure it is built from — visually, into a nearly flat line along the bottom of the chart. County-level data alone is not enough — the sector breakdown is where the shock actually lives.

Evidence 03 — Timing

Payroll data finds out five months late

Unemployment risk is built from claims people file the week they lose work. Employment counts (EMP) come from employer payroll reports on a slower cycle. The shaded band marks how long it took the payroll count to catch up with what claims already showed.

Manufacturing (NAICS 31), Boone County Oct 2022 – Oct 2023
1 mo.for claims-based risk to jump 6×
5 mo.for payroll employment to reflect it
−42%total EMP decline, Feb → Oct 2023

By March 2023, one month after the plant idled, claims-based risk had already jumped six-fold. Payroll employment barely moved — it did not show a comparable decline until July, five months later. Anyone watching only the payroll count would have missed the shock for most of a two-quarter window.

None of this is hidden in an exotic corner of the data. It's the default resolution of the two most-cited labor statistics: state unemployment and employer payroll counts.

Recovering the signal takes claims data at the county-by-sector level, updated on a monthly cycle — which is what the Resilient Economy Lab's unemployment insurance dataset is built to provide, including for states where that resolution has to be modeled rather than observed directly.
