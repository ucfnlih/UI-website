Energy transition use case

Energy employment exposure is not the same as unemployment risk.

Colorado shows why industry exposure and current labor market stress need to be measured separately. Some counties have a much larger or more specialized mining, quarrying, and oil and gas workforce, yet workers there can face lower estimated unemployment risk than workers in counties with less industry exposure.

01 / The finding

Employment exposure tells us where the industry matters. Risk tells us where workers are struggling.

Recent labor market research argues that employment structure alone can miss job loss dynamics. Our county by industry estimates let us compare industry employment exposure with realized unemployment risk.

Rio Blanco is far more specialized in NAICS 21 employment than Mesa, yet Mesa's estimated 2024 unemployment risk is much higher. That gap is the central insight of this use case. Employment share and specialization describe where an industry is important, while unemployment risk describes current labor market stress among workers in that industry.

"Employment exposure and current unemployment risk are not interchangeable."
This is why a county by industry unemployment measure adds information that employment levels, shares, or specialization alone cannot provide.

05 / What to notice

Three patterns, three different policy questions.

The same state contains several very different types of energy labor markets.

High employment exposure, lower current risk

Rio Blanco and Moffat are highly specialized in NAICS 21 employment, yet their estimated 2024 unemployment risk remains relatively low.

Moderate employment exposure, higher current risk

Mesa is less specialized in NAICS 21 employment than several rural counties in the comparison, but it has the highest estimated unemployment risk and the largest absolute increase.

Large industry workforce, moderate risk

Weld has the largest NAICS 21 workforce in the comparison and high specialization, but its estimated risk is below Mesa and Denver.

06 / Method

How we estimate unemployment risk.

This is a descriptive website use case, not a causal estimate of the effect of energy transition.

risk = predicted continued UI claimants / (predicted continued UI claimants + QCEW employment)

Monthly predicted claimants are paired with the corresponding quarter's QCEW employment estimate. The measure reflects estimated continued UI unemployment risk, not the full unemployment rate.

What does "employment exposure" mean here?
We use two standard descriptive labor market measures from QCEW: the share of county employment in NAICS 21 and the NAICS 21 employment location quotient. These measure workforce presence and specialization. They are not a complete measure of community dependence, fiscal exposure, or transition vulnerability.

Why Colorado?
Colorado contains counties with very different mining, oil and gas employment structures, alongside coal transition communities and large diversified metropolitan labor markets. That makes it useful for comparing local structures while holding much of the institutional and policy environment constant.

Selection rule: at least 250 workers in mining, quarrying, and oil and gas extraction and a 2024 employment location quotient above 2. This is only an illustrative screening rule for the demo, not a literature based definition of energy dependence or an energy community. This is an illustrative screen for the website, not a federal definition of an energy community.
